# Consulting team deck: headline spine (v2, titles ≤ 20 words, no semicolons)

Sections in order; each headline is one full sentence stating the takeaway. Method notes (A1…D3) are the appendix deck D entries cited in footnotes.

## 1. Scope and method
1. Calvary funding analysis: consulting team deck *(title)*
2. The analysis rests on 25 public-record tables, with every row carrying a source and a retrieval date.
3. Calvary is compared with 18 provisional DC-area peers, two Los Angeles comparators, and three universes of 163 to 542 organizations.
4. Government share uses the audit basis where audits exist, because the 990 line understated Calvary's contracts by $1.5M in FY21.
5. The limitations are known and bounded, from missing FY24–25 audits and one-year DHS values to partial filings and provisional peers.

## 2. Calvary's trajectory
6. Calvary's revenue grew 59% in real terms from FY19 to FY25, and the FY22 spike was a one-time building award.
7. Government supplied 40–61% of revenue every year since FY15 and most of the growth.
8. The audit basis and the 990 line agree except in FY21, so the FY24–25 figures can be read as audit-equivalent.
9. Calvary ran deficits in two of the last three years, and its cash cushion fell from 7.5 to 4.4 months.
10. Event contributions fell from $538K to $55K in FY25 while net event income turned positive, which suggests a calendar change.
11. Filed institutional grants stayed near $1M a year from 26–29 funders, and GWCF supplied 27% of five years' total.

## 3. Government exposure
12. Calvary's recurring government awards grew from $0.9M (2019) to $4.1M (2024) across OVSJG, HUD CoC and DHS via TCP.
13. Seven streams carry different renewal risks, and the DHS contract and Sisters Rise are the least documented and the riskiest.
14. Calvary recognized 73% (FY24) and 67% (FY25) of its known award ceilings as revenue, a gap the audits would explain.
15. Since pandemic money ended, DC's homeless, PSH and victim-services budgets have held flat to rising through FY2027.
16. The Mayor proposed victim-services and DV cuts in each of the last two springs, and the Council restored them.
17. Calvary holds about 1% of OVSJG awards and 5% of DC's CoC awards, so growth comes from winning projects.
18. OVSJG cuts cost $67–168K, a lost CoC project $366K, a lost DHS/TCP contract $960K, and the combined case $1.31M.

## 4. Peer benchmarking
19. Calvary's 53% government share ranks 11th of 19 DC-area organizations, below every DC DV provider and above the philanthropy-led models.
20. Against the broader sector Calvary is typical, at the 57th percentile in DC and the 46th among national women's organizations.
21. Peers grew past $10M with government (DWC, House of Ruth) or almost without it (Miriam's Kitchen), so size requires neither.
22. Calvary's real growth of 13.8% a year since FY19 trails only My Sister's Place, DWC and DC SAFE among peers.
23. Calvary's 5.2 months of cash in FY24 is mid-pack, between DV providers at one month and Doorways at twelve.
24. Most peers' government share has held since 2017, except Samaritan Inns (down) and DWC and House of Ruth MD (up).
25. Calvary's institutional grants equal 12% of revenue, above all peers except Samaritan Inns, so the headroom is depth per funder.
26. Peers show three replicable paths: replacing government with program revenue, growing private alongside it, or scaling with it.
27. The DV peers draw $3–5M a year each from OVSJG, while Calvary's $1.05M makes it a mid-sized grantee.

## 5. Funder landscape and gaps
28. DMV funders put about $132M a year into DC's homelessness and human-services organizations, 58% of it through donor-advised funds.
29. Ten institutional funders supply 55% of foundation dollars to DC's sector, and six of the 25 largest already fund Calvary.
30. The typical foundation grant to a DC-sector organization is $10–25K, and six-figure grants are the top decile.
31. Fidelity Charitable alone moved $179M of donor-advised money into DC-sector organizations in five years.
32. Most institutional money is general operating or untagged, and the largest mission-tagged funders are Maryland and Virginia foundations.
33. Twenty-three institutional funders give to three or more of Calvary's peers but not Calvary, led by Clark Charitable.
34. Twelve former funders that once gave $255K have filed grant lists since TY2023 with nothing for Calvary.
35. Fifteen of Calvary's current funders have written larger single grants to a peer than to Calvary.
36. Only 44% of the CRM's $10.6M in 2023–26 institutional giving is verifiable in funders' filings.

## 6. Network
37. Calvary sits inside the same funder web as its peers, and most of the best-connected funders already fund it.
38. Calvary shares the most institutional funders with Miriam's Kitchen, DASH and House of Ruth DC.
39. Board interlocks are a thin channel, with one high-confidence Calvary link and seven medium-confidence leads to confirm.

## 7. Pipeline and scoring
40. The pipeline scores 182 candidates on seven weighted dimensions that can be changed in one config file and rerun.
41. Eleven rows reach Tier 1: four current majors, three new foundations, one corporate relationship and three public streams.
42. Peer giving and existing relationships drive Tier 1 scores, while award size separates the foundations that can move the numbers.
43. The 90-day plan gives each Tier 1 target a next step, an owner, a deadline and an application cycle.
44. Moving any weight or the tier cutoff by 0.05 changes ranks but not the ten institutional names in Tiers 1–2.

## 8. Mission expansion
45. Health and workforce funding pools are large and one step from Calvary's programs, while aging, veterans and reentry are small.
46. Each option carries a trade-off, from workforce and behavioral health at modest cost to DV formalization that raises government share.
47. Direct federal competition is thin, with 36 relevant notices, so the realistic channels remain HUD CoC and HUD CPF.

## 9. Need and capacity
48. Calvary's home ward has twice the city's poverty rate and the highest female poverty in DC.
49. DC's homeless count fell from 8,350 (2016) to 5,138 (2025) while Calvary's inventory grew to 124 beds.

## 10. Named comparators
50. DWC grew from $6M to $34M in a decade as government rose to 57% of revenue, on thin cash.
51. The SheWorks parent started at 88% government and is diversifying fast, and SheWorks is a program design to borrow.

## 11. Verdict, recommendations and open items
52. The verdict is moderate-to-high pressure, driven by channel concentration and liquidity rather than by the government share.
53. Five recommendation areas follow: protect and draw the public base, rebuild private revenue, hold a reserve, expand near, mitigate risk.
54. Three documents from Calvary would resolve the three open flags: the audits, the TCP agreements and the FY25 event calendar.
55. The appendix decks carry every chart, table and method note, and the insight register tracks verification for all 36 findings.
