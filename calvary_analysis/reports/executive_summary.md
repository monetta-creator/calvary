# Executive summary: Calvary Women's Services funding analysis

*For Calvary's leadership and board. A pro bono analysis built from public records (IRS filings, funders' own grant lists, DC and federal award records, DC budget documents, Census and HUD data), data as of 2026-10-10. Every figure traces to a chart-data file and a registered public source; the consulting deck and appendix decks carry the full methods. Core peers are provisional.*

## 1. What Calvary has built

Calvary has grown into a system-level provider for single women in DC. Revenue rose 59% in real terms from FY19 to FY25 ($2.49M → $4.97M); housing inventory grew from 68 beds in 2022 to 124 in 2025, now 6.8% of all transitional-housing beds in the District (2.5% three years earlier); three public partners (DC OVSJG, HUD Continuum of Care through TCP, and DC DHS through TCP) fund about $4.1M a year of awards; and 50 foundations and donor-advised sponsors filed grants to Calvary in TY2020–24. All of this in Wards 7 and 8, where poverty runs at 23% and 31% against 15% citywide and a third of renters pay more than half their income in rent.

## 2. The funding picture, honestly

**Public funding is about half of revenue, which is typical.** On the audit basis government supplied 40–61% of revenue in every year since FY15, 53% on average for FY22–24 and 58% in FY25. That places Calvary 11th of 19 DC-area organizations doing similar work and near the median of DC's human-services sector. The share itself is not the issue.

**The shape is what to plan around.** The money arrives through three streams, two of them through one intermediary: OVSJG grants ($1.05M in FY26), HUD CoC renewals via TCP ($1.77M in FY2025) and the DHS contract via TCP ($1.42M in FY2024, the only year that is public). TCP carries 74% of the base.

**Private support has held steady and is concentrated.** Grants named in funders' own filings have run at $0.64–1.17M a year from the same 26–29 funders; the Greater Washington Community Foundation (including the time-limited Health Equity Fund) is 27% of five years of filed grants, and the top five are 63%. Donor-advised sponsors add about $1.1M over five years.

**FY25 thinned the cushion.** After a strong FY24, FY25 brought a $607K operating deficit, a $483K drop in event income (which may be a calendar change: net event income turned positive) and a cash cushion of 4.4 months, down from 7.5 in FY21.

**DC's pools have held, with cuts proposed each spring.** The OVSJG Victim Services line is approved at $55–56M through FY2027; the Mayor proposed cuts of $5.8M (FY26) and $5.6M (FY27) and the Council restored both. DHS homeless-services and PSH budgets are flat to rising. Actual OVSJG spending runs 9–14% below approved.

**Calvary may be leaving awarded money on the table.** It recognized about two-thirds of its known award ceilings as revenue in FY24 and FY25 ($2.62M of $3.6M; $2.89M of $4.3M). Ceilings are not revenue, and the DHS value is carried forward from FY2024, so this is a question rather than a finding; the FY24 and FY25 audits and the TCP subgrant agreements would settle it.

**What a cut would do.** The cut proposed to OVSJG last year costs Calvary about $67K; a 25% OVSJG cut $168K; a 15% CoC cut $178K; losing one CoC project $366K; losing the DHS contract through TCP $960K (19% of revenue). In the combined case Calvary would hold under two months of cash after a year even with half of the lost stream's costs avoided. The headline cuts are survivable; the TCP-channel contract is the cloud to plan for.

**Verdict:** moderate-to-high funding pressure, driven by channel concentration and liquidity rather than by the government share.

## 3. The opportunities

- **Peers show the path.** Organizations with Calvary's profile have grown on more than one model. The closest fit is N Street Village's: grow private support alongside a stable public base (~40% government, 8 months of cash). Samaritan Inns replaced government with program revenue; Miriam's Kitchen grew on philanthropy; DWC in Los Angeles scaled more than five-fold with government rising to 57%.
- **The headroom is depth, not count.** Calvary already works its foundation funders harder than most peers (42 institutional funders; grants equal to 12% of revenue versus 6–8% at N Street Village and House of Ruth DC). The levers are larger multi-year grants, major donors and donor-advised funds (peers raise 40–50% of filed grants through DAFs; Calvary 25%), and earned revenue.
- **The money exists.** DMV funders put about $132M a year into DC's sector; 23 institutional funders give to three or more of Calvary's peers without yet funding Calvary, led by Clark Charitable ($7.8M to five peers, median grant $250K); 12 former funders have lapsed; 15 current funders write larger checks to peers.
- **Calvary is already inside the funder web.** It shares 17 institutional funders with Miriam's Kitchen and 14 each with DASH and House of Ruth DC. Introductions run through those peers and shared funders; board overlaps are a thin channel in public filings.
- **Nearby pools open without changing the mission.** Formalizing workforce (a SheWorks-style cohort inside Step Up DC) and behavioral health (a credential or billing partner) opens $30M and $21M a year of DMV grant language and public programs; a health partnership with an FQHC is the largest medium-distance pool ($84M a year).

## 4. What we suggest

1. **Draw every awarded dollar and protect the public base (first 30 days):** reconcile ceilings to revenue for FY24–26, confirm the DHS contract and the Sisters Rise/EHV status with TCP, defend the three CoC renewals and scope a PSH expansion project, and put the OVSJG budget season on the calendar.
2. **Rebuild private revenue on three tracks (0–12 months):** multi-year general-operating upgrades with current majors (GWCF, with a Health Equity Fund successor conversation; Cafritz, deadlines March 1, July 1, November 1; Abell; Fowler; United Way NCA; Bank of America); open Clark Charitable, Schattner, Cohen, Bernstein and The Washington Home through peer introductions; re-engage Many Hands (January window), the World Bank Community Connections Fund and Jane Bancroft Robinson; restore the event line; start a named major-gifts and DAF program; convert the strongest corporate relationships to multi-year.
3. **Hold a six-month reserve** (about $2.8M at FY25 spending) and close the FY25 gap in the FY26 budget.
4. **Expand near first:** workforce and the behavioral-health credential decision, then the clinic partnership; DV formalization only if a higher public share is acceptable; families would be a mission change.
5. **Manage government risk within government:** a direct DHS line alongside TCP, HUD CPF and DHS PSH as channels, better contract terms, monthly draw-down tracking, and an advocacy calendar.

The target arithmetic: private revenue from about $2.1M (FY25) to about $3.0M in three years brings the public share to roughly half at flat public funding, restores a surplus and rebuilds the reserve.
