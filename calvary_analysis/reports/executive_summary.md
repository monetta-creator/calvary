# Executive summary: Calvary Women's Services funding analysis

*For Calvary's leadership and board. A pro bono analysis built from public records (IRS filings, funders' own grant lists, DC and federal award records, DC budget documents, Census and HUD data), data as of 2026-10-10 (revised 2026-10-11). Every figure traces to a chart-data file and a registered public source; the consulting deck and appendix decks carry the full methods. Core peers are provisional.*

## 1. What Calvary has built

Calvary has grown into a system-level provider for single women in DC. Revenue rose from $2.49M in FY19 to $4.97M in FY25 in nominal dollars, +59% after inflation; housing inventory grew from 68 beds in 2022 to 124 in 2025 across transitional, permanent supportive and rapid-rehousing programs, and its 94 transitional beds are 6.8% of the District's (its 45 transitional beds were 2.5% in 2022); three public partners (DC OVSJG, HUD Continuum of Care through TCP, and DC DHS through TCP) fund about $4.1M a year of awards; and 50 foundations and donor-advised sponsors filed grants to Calvary in TY2020–24. All of this in Wards 7 and 8, where poverty runs at 23% and 31% against 15% citywide and a third of renters pay more than half their income in rent.

## 2. The funding picture, honestly

**Public funding is about half of revenue, which is typical.** On the audit basis government supplied 40–61% of revenue in every year since FY15, 53% on average for FY22–24 and 58% in FY25. That places Calvary 11th of 19 DC-area organizations doing similar work. Among DC human-services organizations that report government revenue Calvary sits at the 57th percentile; against all filers (no government line counted as 0%) it is in the top quarter (84th in DC, 74th nationally). The share itself is not the issue.

**The shape is what to plan around.** The money arrives through three streams, two of them through one intermediary: OVSJG grants ($1.05M in FY26), HUD CoC renewals via TCP ($1.77M in FY2025) and the DHS contract via TCP ($1.42M in FY2024, the only year that is public). TCP carries about three-quarters of the base (78% of the $4.1M 2024 award base).

**Private support has held steady and is concentrated.** Grants named in funders' own filings have run at $0.64–1.17M a year from the same 26–29 funders; the Greater Washington Community Foundation (including the time-limited Health Equity Fund) is 27% of five years of filed grants, and the top five are 63%. Donor-advised sponsors add about $1.1M over five years.

**FY25 ran a deficit.** After a strong FY24, FY25 brought a $607K operating deficit and a cash cushion of 4.4 months, down from 7.5 in FY21. Event receipts held near $0.53–0.60M (FY24 $596K, FY25 $533K); the FY25 990 presented gala receipts as fundraising-event income (line 8a, with $259K of direct costs netted) instead of contributions (line 1c).

**DC's pools have held, with cuts proposed each spring.** The OVSJG Victim Services line is approved at $55–56M through FY2027; the Mayor proposed cuts of $5.8M (FY26) and $5.6M (FY27) and the Council restored both, though the FY27 restoration is $5.1M one-time plus $0.5M recurring, so the FY2028 base is back on the table next spring. DHS homeless-services and PSH budgets are flat to rising. Actual OVSJG spending runs 9–14% below approved.

**Calvary may be leaving awarded money on the table.** It recognized about two-thirds of its known award ceilings as revenue in FY24 and FY25 ($2.62M of $3.6M; $2.89M of $4.3M). Ceilings are not revenue, and the DHS value is carried forward from FY2024, so this stays a question for DHS. For CoC the audited FY22 and FY23 single audits already show the gap: CoC spending ran at 28% (FY22) and 58% (FY23) of the prior year's CoC award ceilings, so it is specific to that stream and predates FY24. Reconcile CoC draw-down first, then DHS; the FY24 and FY25 audits and the TCP subgrant agreements would settle the rest.

**What a cut would do.** Each loss is a range across three ways of splitting FY25 government revenue across streams. The cut proposed to OVSJG last year costs Calvary about $67K–$100K; a 25% OVSJG cut $168K–$250K; a 15% CoC cut $99K–$207K; losing one CoC project $204K–$424K; losing the DHS contract through TCP about $0.65M–$1.13M a year (13–23% of revenue). The DHS figure depends most on the split. In the combined case (about $1.05M–$1.47M, 21–30% of revenue) Calvary would hold 1.5–2.0 months of cash after a year even with half of the lost stream's costs avoided. The headline cuts are survivable; the TCP channel is the cloud to plan for, since CoC and DHS together are $1.8–2.2M under every split. Two federal dates sit in that channel: HUD's FY2026 CoC awards (expected about December 2026, litigation pending) and HUD's 29 September 2026 notice ending the EHV program behind Sisters Rise.

**Verdict:** moderate-to-high funding pressure, driven by channel concentration and liquidity rather than by the government share.

## 3. The opportunities

- **Peers show the path.** Organizations with Calvary's profile have grown on more than one model. The closest fit is N Street Village's: grow private support alongside a stable public base (~40% government, 8 months of cash). Samaritan Inns replaced government with program revenue; Miriam's Kitchen grew on philanthropy; DWC in Los Angeles scaled more than five-fold with government rising to 57%.
- **The headroom is depth, not count.** Calvary already works its foundation funders harder than most peers (42 institutional funders; grants equal to 12% of revenue versus 6–8% at N Street Village and House of Ruth DC). The levers are larger multi-year grants, major donors and donor-advised funds (peers raise 40–50% of filed grants through DAFs; Calvary 25%), and earned revenue.
- **The money exists.** DMV funders put about $132M a year into DC's sector. Of the 23 institutional funders that give to three or more of Calvary's peers without yet funding Calvary, six are giving channels rather than grantmakers, Clark Charitable has closed and Venable is closed to new applicants; 15 choose their own grantees. The firmest new name is Schattner (four peers, Tier 1, invitation-only); two smaller family foundations also fund four peers, and the three-peer names depend on the provisional peer set. Eleven former funders, who once gave $0.16M, have lapsed; 15 current funders that also fund two or more peers write larger checks to peers.
- **Calvary is already inside the funder web.** It shares 17 institutional funders with Miriam's Kitchen and 14 each with DASH and House of Ruth DC. Introductions run through those peers and shared funders; public filings show no high-confidence board links, so boards are a thin channel.
- **Nearby pools open without changing the mission.** Formalizing workforce (a SheWorks-style cohort inside Step Up DC) and behavioral health (a credential or billing partner) opens $30M and $21M a year of DMV grant language and public programs; a health partnership with an FQHC is the largest medium-distance pool ($84M a year).

## 4. What we suggest

1. **Draw every awarded dollar and protect the public base (first 30 days):** reconcile CoC draw-down first (the audited years already show the gap there), then DHS, for FY24–26; confirm the DHS contract with TCP and what the end of EHV means for Sisters Rise; follow the three CoC renewals through HUD's FY2026 awards and scope a PSH expansion project; and put the OVSJG budget season on the calendar.
2. **Rebuild private revenue on three tracks (0–12 months):** funder-by-funder upgrades with current majors (peer-level multi-year asks to Cafritz, deadlines March 1, July 1, November 1, and Fowler; two GWCF conversations, a Health Equity Fund successor and core operating support; steward and renew Abell, which is invitation-only; then United Way NCA and Bank of America); an invitation to Schattner through a named peer or co-funder (no LOI) once the peer set is confirmed; a staff conversation with Jane Bancroft Robinson ahead of its next RFP and calls to other lapsed funders; start a named major-gifts and DAF program; convert the strongest corporate relationships to multi-year. Sequence the asks for a six-person fundraising team: government in month 1, two current-funder conversations a fortnight in month 2, introductions in month 3.
3. **Hold a six-month reserve** (about $2.8M at FY25 spending) and close the FY25 gap in the FY26 budget.
4. **Expand near first:** workforce (its first cost is funding the second workforce position, unfunded in FY2026) and the behavioral-health credential decision, then the clinic partnership; DV formalization only if a higher public share is acceptable; families would be a mission change.
5. **Manage government risk within government:** a direct DHS line alongside TCP, HUD CPF and DHS PSH as channels, better contract terms, monthly draw-down tracking, and an advocacy calendar.

The target arithmetic: private revenue from about $2.1M (FY25) to about $3.0M in three years (institutional upgrades and peer-funder introductions +$0.5M, major-donor and DAF giving +$0.3M, earned or billable program revenue +$0.1–0.3M if pursued; events held near $0.5M, so no restoration component) brings the public share to roughly half at flat public funding, restores a surplus and rebuilds the reserve.

## 5. What comes next

Nine Tier 1 targets (five foundations, three government streams and one corporate partner) carry a next step, an owner role and a deadline in the 90-day plan. Two documents from Calvary would sharpen every number: the FY24 and FY25 audited statements and the TCP subgrant agreements for FY2023–FY2026; the FY26 gala date is a small check.
